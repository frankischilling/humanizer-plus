# Editing by context

The examples below use invented names and scenarios. Check the audience, purpose, and destination before applying a pattern.

## Keep meaningful qualifications

**Before**

> The pilot included 18 households. It shows how often those households lost power. It does not establish a townwide rate.

**After**

> The pilot measured outages in 18 households. The townwide outage rate remains unknown.

The sample size and the limit on the finding both matter. The shorter version works when the draft supplies no other townwide estimate. A casual “perhaps” in an essay may also carry the author's intended uncertainty.

## Keep attribution where it matters

**Before**

> The manufacturer's handbook identifies the oven as having four racks. Two reviewers said the door was hard to open.

**After**

> The oven has four racks, according to the handbook. Two reviewers said the door was hard to open.

Keep the handbook citation. If the same specification is already cited inline, the prose can simply state the rack count. The two reviewers remain two reviewers.

In a professional bio, a speaker may want to name interviews. In an encyclopedia entry, a list of outlets may add little unless the coverage itself matters. A sourced award name and date can be worth keeping in either setting.

## Open with the subject

In an article called “Garden sheds in Mossbank,” a lead such as “Garden sheds in Mossbank refers to the garden sheds located in Mossbank” just repeats the title. If the source says the council built six sheds along the canal in 1932, lead with that.

A list or directory can open with a sentence that defines its scope. In a product guide, a title such as “Northbank Sync” can be an official name worth repeating.

## Match the destination's formatting

Suppose the draft includes this heading:

```markdown
## Visiting hours
```

Keep it in a README. In a wikitext draft, the corresponding section heading is `== Visiting hours ==`. Apply the conversion when the user asks for a wikitext-ready edit. During a file copyedit, flag the mismatch if markup edits are outside the request.

Check heading levels and duplicate titles against the surrounding site. Keep title case in a publication that uses it. Keep a table when readers need to compare hours across branches. A sentence is easier to read when it states one branch's hours.

In wiki material, confirm template names, categories, and named references before changing them. Keep real maintenance banners and required disclosures. If an accidental draft carries what looks like a reviewer decision or submission banner, flag it for the author to check in the page history.

## Keep unknowns visible

A finished invitation with “RSVP at [booking link]” needs the booking link. Fill it from the user's materials when available. Otherwise flag it in a note and leave the placeholder visible in the draft. Keep the same placeholder in a reusable invitation template.

An opaque citation token pasted after a number calls for a source check. If verification is in scope, compare the cited work's title and authors with the DOI record, then check whether the work supports the claim. A link to the publisher's search page does not verify the claim. A book without an online copy may still be a good source. Ask for a page or section when a reader needs to find a specific passage.

Keep original link targets and citation markers during a prose-only file edit. Report a mismatch with enough detail for the author to find it. When the user supplies the verified correction and asks to apply it, change the target and keep the citation with the relevant claim.

## Write for the reader of the change

**Before**

> In response to the feedback, I carefully revised the schedule to improve accuracy and clarity while preserving all other times. The Sunday bus now leaves at 8:30.

**After**

> Moved the Sunday bus departure to 8:30.

An edit summary or commit message needs the actual change. In a notice sent to passengers, it may be useful to say that the weekday timetable still applies. Keep a policy reference if it explains a real decision, and keep concrete questions in a review reply.

## Preserve the writer's choices

> I waited at the stop for ages — long enough to get to know the dog next door. The April–June timetable is taped to my fridge now.

The aside and date range work as written. The same judgment applies to an email greeting, a quotation with curly punctuation, or a repeated line that gives a story its rhythm.
